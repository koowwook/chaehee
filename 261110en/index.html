💡 Well-diversified ETFs fell less, again

The S&P 500 ETF fell 6%, far less than single stocks (-25% to -38%).

So when you pick an ETF, just check what's inside it. PART 4 shows how.

When expectations ran far ahead of earnings, as in the 2000 dot-com bubble, the Nasdaq took 15 years to get back to its old high.

CASE 1

🔴 Cut back

Money you need within 5 years is in stockshome deposit, a move, tuition
Count back from the date you need it, and move it into savings in set amounts on set dates.
e.g. $20,000 for a home deposit due in a year → move $5,000 a month for the next 4 months
You bought stocks with borrowed moneymargin loans, credit lines
Stop buying and pay down the debt first. If prices keep falling, your broker can sell your shares for you (a margin call).
e.g. $10,000 bought on margin → put your paycheck and spare cash toward paying it off, not more stock
Your reason for buying is gonerevenue and profit are falling (see PART 2)
Consider trimming that stock. Don't sell just because the price dropped.
You're so concentrated in one industry you can't sleep
Trim only down to the limit in the "How much is too much?" guide below.
e.g. $15,000 of $30,000 in chips (50%) → sell just $6,000 to get down to $9,000 (30%). Move that money into other industries or an index ETF
For all · Don't sell everything in one day. Sell set amounts on set dates.
⚠️ The bigger your loss, the more careful you need to be with chat groups that promise to win back your losses. In Korea alone, investment-scam losses hit ₩181.7 billion in Q1 2026, and 3 in 4 victims were in their 50s or older.
CASE 2

🟡 Hold and wait

You have no spare cash to add
Keep what you have. If you invest a set amount every month, keep going.
You have spare cash, but you already own a lot of that stock or industry
Don't buy more of it. Put the spare cash into other industries or an index ETF, or keep it in cash and wait.
e.g. $12,000 of $30,000 in chips (40%) and $5,000 of spare cash
→ buying more chips would push it to 49%
→ hold the chips even at a loss, and put the $5,000 somewhere else like an S&P 500 ETF, or keep it in cash
For all · Turn off app alerts and check only once a month, on a set day.

PART 4

New to stocks?
Should I buy ETFs now?

Four steps for getting started

STEP 1 How much can I buy?

In a sell-off, decide "how far will I go?" before "how much should I buy?" Your limit is what's left after taking out your emergency fund, money you'll need within 5 years, and debt you should pay off first.

Your limit is an amount you could live with even if prices fall further. Work it out with the PART 5 calculator.

STEP 2 Which ETF should I start with?

If you're new, start by looking at index ETFs that hold many industries at once, like the S&P 500 or the total U.S. market. In this crash, the S&P 500 fell 6% from its high, while the chip-only ETF fell 12% in a single day.

⭕ Start here1–2 broad index ETFs that hold many industries
❌ Skip at first2x or 3x leveraged, inverse, and single-theme ETFs

STEP 3 All at once, or in steps?

①

Monthly investingEasiest

Put in the same amount on the same day each month. No guessing when to buy, and when prices fall, the same money buys more shares.

Good for Starting from your paycheck without a lump sum, or if drops would keep you up at night
e.g. $500 on the 25th of every month
②

Part now + the rest monthlyIf you have a lump sum

Invest part of your lump sum now and spread the rest over a few months. You use some of today's lower prices and still have cash if prices fall further.

Good for Anyone with a lump sum they can leave for 5+ years
e.g. $12,000 → $4,000 now (one third), then the other $8,000 at $1,000 a month for 8 months
③

Buy at set price levelsIf you check often

Decide in advance how far below the high you'll buy, and buy once each time a level is hit.

Good for People who check the market often and can stick to the rules
e.g. When an index ETF is down 10% · 15% · 20% from its high, invest 30% · 30% · 40% of your money
📌 What about investing it all at once? When Vanguard compared the two using past data, investing all at once beat spreading it over a year about 2 out of 3 times. But when markets fell sharply, spreading it out lost less. So all-at-once wins on returns, and monthly investing wins on peace of mind. If you're starting in a sell-off, option ② can be a middle ground.
I'm starting from my paycheck, no lump sum① Monthly
I have a lump sum but drops would make me anxious① Over 6–12 mo
I have a lump sum and can leave it 5+ years② Part + monthly
I check the market often and can stick to rules③ Price levels

STEP 4 What if it drops more after I buy?

  • If it keeps fallingBuy only at your next planned step. No unplanned extra buys.
  • If it bounces backDon't rush to put in the rest. Keep monthly buys on schedule, and wait for your next price level.
  • When you hit your limitStop, even if prices keep falling. After that, just keep investing from your paycheck.
  • Checking inTurn off app alerts and look once a month. Review your mix once or twice a year.
  • When you sellNot because it fell. When the date you need the money gets close, move it to cash in steps on set dates.
🗓️ Example: starting with $12,000 (option ②)
Buy 1 · now$4,000
Buys 2–9 · 25th of each month$1,000 each
If it drops in betweenStick to the schedule
Once all $12,000 is inStop, review in 6 months
